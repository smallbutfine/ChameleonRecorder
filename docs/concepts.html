Audio Recording Concepts — In Plain Language

This page explains the ideas behind ChameleonRecorder in terms a live sound engineer, rehearsal studio owner, or musician can understand. No programming knowledge required.

1. What Is Multitrack Recording?

Multitrack recording means capturing every input channel as a separate WAV file. If you have 16 microphones on a drum kit, you get 16 individual WAV files — one for kick, one for snare, one for each tom, overheads, room mics, and so on.

Why Separate Files Matter

Each channel is its own file. You can adjust the level, EQ, compression, and effects on each microphone independently during mixing. If everything were recorded as a single stereo mix, you could never fix a buried snare or a boomy kick without re-recording.

What You Get

One WAV file per input channel, all starting at the same instant. Drop them into any DAW (Reaper, Pro Tools, Logic, Ardour, etc.) and they line up perfectly — every track is sample-synced from the first sample.

Think of it like this: a stereo recording is a photograph of your mix. A multitrack recording is the raw ingredients — every instrument on its own plate, ready for the chef to season however they want.
Key point: ChameleonRecorder records every armed input channel to its own WAV file. You choose which channels to record from the web remote. Unused channels can be left out to save disk space.

3. USB Audio Interfaces

ChameleonRecorder works with USB audio interfaces — the boxes that turn microphones and instruments into digital audio. But not all USB interfaces are the same.

Class-Compliant vs. Proprietary

TypeWhat It MeansExamples
Class-Compliant Follows the USB Audio Class standard. Works with any computer or device without installing drivers. Plug it in and it works — like a USB keyboard or mouse. Behringer UMC1820, UMC204HD, XR18, X32, MOTU AVB series, Focusrite Scarlett (most models), RME Babyface, Zoom F-series

Important: ChameleonRecorder only supports class-compliant USB audio interfaces. If your interface requires a driver download from the manufacturer's website, it will not work. Check the manufacturer's specs for "USB Audio Class Compliant" or "class compliant."

4. JACK Audio Server

JACK (JACK Audio Connection Kit) is the audio routing engine that ChameleonRecorder uses to move audio from your USB interface to the recording software. Think of it as the nervous system that connects everything together.

What JACK Does

JACK takes the digital audio stream from your USB interface and routes it to the recording application (jack_capture). It handles all the timing, buffering, and channel routing so that every sample arrives at the right place at the right time.

Why Low Latency Matters

Latency is the delay between sound going in and the system processing it. JACK achieves much lower latency than a typical computer's audio stack because it bypasses the operating system's audio mixer and talks directly to the hardware. This means no lag, no dropouts, and reliable recording even under heavy load.

Master / Slave Concept

When you use multiple USB interfaces, one of them must be the timing reference — the "master" clock. All other interfaces become "slaves" and follow the master's timing. ChameleonRecorder automatically picks the interface with the most channels as the master.

RoleInterfaceWhat It Does
MasterInterface with most channels (e.g., UMC1820)Sets the sample rate and clock. All other interfaces sync to it.
SlaveAdditional interfaces (e.g., second UMC1820, ADA8200)Follows the master's clock via ADAT or drift compensation.
How ChameleonRecorder uses JACK: On boot, the system scans all USB audio cards, picks the one with the most channels as the JACK master, starts the JACK server at 48 kHz, and binds additional interfaces via alsa_in with adaptive drift compensation. The jack_capture application then records all channels to individual WAV files.

5. Clock Sync — Keeping Everything in Time

When you record with multiple audio interfaces, each one has its own internal clock. Even if they start at the same time, they will slowly drift apart — just like two watches that are a few seconds different after a month. This drift causes phase problems and timing errors in your multitrack.

Why Interfaces Drift

Every digital audio device has a crystal oscillator that determines its sample rate. No two crystals are exactly the same — one might run at 48,000 Hz and another at 48,003 Hz. Over a 10-minute recording, that tiny difference adds up to several samples of drift, which means your tracks slowly shift out of sync.

Solutions for Clock Sync

MethodHow It WorksAccuracy
ADAT Lightpipe A single optical cable carries both audio and clock signal from one device to another. The receiving device locks to the sending device's clock. Sample-accurate. Sample-accurate (0 samples drift)
Word Clock (BNC) A dedicated coax cable carries only the clock signal between devices. Each device locks to the word clock generator. Sample-accurate. Sample-accurate (0 samples drift)
Drift Compensation When no hardware sync is available, JACK's alsa_in continuously measures the drift between interfaces and adjusts the resampling ratio to compensate. Not sample-accurate, but good enough for most rehearsal and live recording situations. Approximate (a few samples of drift)
ChameleonRecorder and ADAT: When a Behringer UMC1820 is detected with a valid ADAT signal on its optical input, ChameleonRecorder automatically switches it to external ADAT clock sync. This gives you sample-accurate recording across multiple units without any manual configuration.
For critical studio work where every sample counts, always use ADAT or word clock. For rehearsal and live demos, drift compensation is usually transparent — you won't hear the difference.

6. Recording Format — 24-bit / 48 kHz WAV

ChameleonRecorder records uncompressed WAV files at 24-bit resolution and 48 kHz sample rate. Here's what that means and why it matters:

24-Bit — Dynamic Range

Bit depth determines how much dynamic range your recording can capture. 24-bit gives you 144 dB of theoretical dynamic range — far more than any microphone or preamp can actually deliver. This means you can record quietly and still have plenty of headroom. In practice, 24-bit means you never have to worry about noise floor or quantization distortion, even at very low levels.

48 kHz — The Video Standard

48 kHz is the standard sample rate for video production, film, and television. If your recording will ever be synced to video, 48 kHz is the correct choice. It also captures the full audible frequency range (up to 24 kHz) with plenty of margin. 44.1 kHz is used for CD audio, but 48 kHz is the professional standard for multitrack recording.

File Sizes

Uncompressed WAV files are large. Here's roughly how much space you need per minute of recording:

ChannelsPer MinutePer HourExample
2 (stereo)~33 MB~2 GBAcoustic guitar + vocal
8~132 MB~8 GBFull drum kit

Storage tip: A 64 GB USB 3.0 flash drive holds about 4 hours of 16-track recording. For longer sessions, use a larger drive or an SSD. Always use USB 3.0 (blue ports) — USB 2.0 can drop samples during multitrack recording.
24-bit vs 16-bit: 16-bit (CD quality) gives you 96 dB of dynamic range. That sounds like a lot, but in practice, 24-bit gives you about 48 dB more headroom above the noise floor. This means you can set your gain conservatively and never worry about clipping or noise. Always record in 24-bit.

7. The Fallback Wi-Fi Hotspot

ChameleonRecorder includes a built-in Wi-Fi hotspot so you can control it from your phone or tablet even when there's no Wi-Fi router available. This is essential for stage use, rehearsal studios, and remote recording locations.

Why It Matters

Most venues don't have Wi-Fi, or the Wi-Fi is unreliable. The fallback hotspot means ChameleonRecorder is completely self-contained — it creates its own network and serves the web remote from itself. No router, no internet, no IT department required.

How It Works

On boot, ChameleonRecorder looks for a known Wi-Fi network for 25 seconds. If it doesn't find one, it creates its own access point with SSID ChameleonRecorder and password chameleon123. Connect your phone to this network, then open http://10.42.0.1:8080 in any browser.

DetailValue
Network name (SSID)ChameleonRecorder
Passwordchameleon123
Web remote URLhttp://10.42.0.1:8080
TriggerNo known Wi-Fi found within 25 seconds of boot

Security Considerations

  • Network isolation: The hotspot is a standalone network — devices connected to it cannot reach the internet or your other networks.
  • Password protection: The default password is chameleon123. Change it in the configuration if you're in a shared space.
  • No internet access: The hotspot does not route traffic to the internet. It only serves the web remote control page.
  • Temporary: The hotspot only activates when no known Wi-Fi is found. Once you connect to a known network, the hotspot shuts down automatically.
Pro tip: If you use ChameleonRecorder at a venue with unreliable Wi-Fi, the fallback hotspot is your safety net. You can always control the recorder from your phone, no matter what.

8. GPIO Controls — Physical Buttons and LED Status

The Raspberry Pi has general-purpose input/output (GPIO) pins that ChameleonRecorder uses for physical controls. This means you can record, stop, and delete takes without needing a phone, tablet, or computer.

Why Physical Buttons Matter

In a studio or on stage, fumbling with a phone app is slow and unreliable. A physical button you can press without looking is faster, more reliable, and works even when your phone is dead or in another room. It's the difference between a hardware recorder and an app.

LED Status Indicator

A status LED gives you instant visual feedback: solid green means ready, blinking red means recording, and off means powered down. You can glance at the Pi from across the room and know its state without unlocking your phone.

Setup: GPIO controls are optional. If you don't connect buttons, ChameleonRecorder works exactly the same — you just use the web remote instead. The GPIO pins are there for convenience, not requirement.

Reading the diagram: Your microphones plug into the audio interface. The interface sends digital audio over USB to the Raspberry Pi. JACK receives it, keeps everything in sync, and passes it to jack_capture, which writes each channel to its own WAV file on the USB flash drive. The whole chain is automatic — you just press record.

Step by Step

  1. Microphone → Audio Interface: Your mics or instruments connect to the interface's preamps. The interface converts analog sound to digital audio.
  2. Audio Interface → USB: The digital audio travels over USB to the Raspberry Pi. Because the interface is class-compliant, no driver is needed — the Pi recognizes it immediately.
  3. USB → JACK: JACK takes the incoming audio and routes it. If you have multiple interfaces, JACK keeps them all in sync using ADAT, word clock, or drift compensation.
  4. JACK → jack_capture: The jack_capture application receives every channel from JACK and writes each one to a separate WAV file. It runs at 24-bit / 48 kHz.
  5. WAV → USB Stick: The WAV files are saved directly to the USB flash drive at /media/usb0/. Each file is named with a timestamp so you know exactly when it was recorded.
